Llamas are domesticated South American camelids that belong to the camel family. They are closely related to alpacas, guanacos, and vicunas. Llamas originated in the Andes of South America and are especially associated with Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, Chile, and Argentina.

Llamas are well adapted to high mountain environments. Their thick wool helps protect them from cold temperatures, while their padded feet help them walk across rocky ground. They can live in areas where water and vegetation are limited.

Llamas are herbivores. They eat grasses, shrubs, leaves, and other plants. Like other camelids, they chew their food, bring it back up, and chew it again. This process helps them digest tough plant material efficiently.

People have raised llamas for thousands of years. One of their most important traditional uses is carrying loads across mountain paths. Llamas can carry moderate packs and are useful in places where vehicles cannot travel easily. They are also kept for their wool, which can be used to make clothing, blankets, ropes, and other woven products.

Llama wool comes in many colors, including white, brown, gray, black, and mixed shades. The quality and texture of the fiber can vary between animals. Some llamas are kept mainly as pack animals, while others are raised for fiber or companionship.

Llamas are social animals and usually live in groups. They communicate through ear positions, body movements, humming sounds, and other vocalizations. A llama may spit as a warning or as a response to conflict, although this behavior is not usually directed at people when the animal is calm and handled properly.

Llamas are generally intelligent and can learn simple tasks. They are sometimes used in animal-assisted activities and as guard animals for groups of sheep or goats. Their alert nature may help them notice predators and warn other animals.

Llamas should be treated respectfully and provided with suitable food, clean water, shelter, space to move, and veterinary care. Their uses today include trekking, farming, fiber production, conservation education, and companionship.
